For true random number generators, or TRNGs, there are a variety of types of noise sources that they can use to generate their random values. In particular, there are approximately five or six types, with some overlap between them. These sources are as follows: electric noise, chaos noise, jitter noise, metastability, quantum noise, and hardware-based noise.

Within electric noise, there are three subtypes, consisting of thermal, shot, and flicker, or 1/f noise. Thermal noise is created when electrons collide with “thermally excited atoms” and is present in all devices possessing resistance (Cao et al.). In comparison, shot noise is created “when charge carriers traverse a gap” in electric current, while flicker noise “originates from carrier number fluctuations (CNF) in MOSFETs”. MOSFETS are a type of semiconductor transistor.

Following electric noise, the secondary entropy source of chaos noise is “based on a deterministic chaotic system or circuit” (Cao et al.). It uses a chaotic map, which is built using “mathematical functions that generate a highly arbitrary pattern based on the initial seed value” (Naik et al.). The chaotic map has two types, continuous or discrete, that both use a clock signal as a seed. Continuous types use analog devices for seeding, whereas discrete types use a digital circuit IC.

The third type of entropy source, jitter noise, is “the difference between the actual clock and the ideal clock in the time domain” (Cao et al.). This essentially means it measures the difference in timing between what should theoretically be the output and the actual output. There are a variety of sources of this phenomenon, such as “equipment noise, power noise, external interference, \[and\] load changes”. In TRNGs that use jitter as an entropy source, a common method to access it is through ring oscillators. This method involves having two ring oscillators, each with a separate jitter clock. The TRNG will use one jitter clock to obtain random values from the other (Sivaraman et al.).

Another method of utilizing entropy for a TRNG implements metastability, or a state prior to having a system in its typical stable state, like between 0 and 1 in binary. This is present “in cross-coupled inverters, latches, D-Flip-flops (DFFs), and SRAMs” (Cao et al.). It is challenging for there to be a level of consistency with this noise source, so it is necessary to employ a large amount of post-processing in TRNGs accessing metastability.

Other than the prior methods mentioned above, quantum noise is another viable option. This entropy source involves the utilization of quantum mechanics to drive the device, and TRNGs that apply it are often known as QRNGs instead, or quantum random number generators. As an example, a QRNG could measure the difference in quantum noise signals produced by a beam of laser light that was split between ports with and without a vacuum (Trifonov 1).

Finally, hardware-based noise used in a TRNG is connected to the other sources that harness physical processes for their entropy, meaning that electric noise, jitter noise, and metastability are essentially subtypes of this source, so long as they are measuring physical processes.
